Why Skill Swaps Stall (And How to Never Get Ghosted)

Grit&Gigs · October 3, 2026 · 7 min read

You offer to design a logo if someone will teach you Excel. It feels safer than money, because nobody gets invoiced and nobody can sue you.

Then three days pass. Nothing was wrong. Nobody lied. The other person just quietly stopped replying.

That is the most common way a barter dies, and it is not a dramatic failure. It is silence.

So the problem was never finding someone. The problem is finishing.

Where the trade actually dies

A swap has three moments where it can stop, and only one of them is about skills.

1. The vague request

"Looking for someone to help with my business." Nobody replies, because nobody knows what you are offering or what you want in return. Vague is not modest here. Vague is invisible.

2. The mutual enthusiasm

Both sides agree enthusiastically. This feels like progress. It is not progress, it is the part that is easiest.

3. The missing definition of done

Nobody said what finished looked like. One person thinks a logo means three options, the other thought it meant three revisions of one option, delivered, approved, installed.

Three weeks later neither person is in the wrong, and the swap is dead.

Five rules that get a swap finished

1. Write down what done means, in one sentence

Not a brief. One sentence you would both agree with. "Five logo concepts as PNG plus one editable file." That sentence prevents ninety percent of the arguments that happen later.

2. Make the first step tiny

Ask for something small before anything large. One shared document. One fifteen-minute call. Small completions are what create momentum; big ones create postponement.

3. Agree the timeline out loud, and keep it short

A swap is not a project. Two weeks is a long time when no money is involved. If it cannot be done in two weeks, it should not be a barter.

4. Ask for the other half first

This is the least comfortable rule. If you deliver everything and then wait, you have no leverage. Ask them to send their half first, or trade in steps.

5. Use somewhere that records both sides

Goodwill is not a system. When delivery is tracked per person, a stalled swap stops being a private guessing game and becomes something visible to both sides.

What a real barter request looks like

Here is the difference between a request that sits there and one that gets matched.

Weak: "Need someone for my business, can help with marketing stuff."

No skill offered. No skill wanted. No timeline. Nothing to reply to.

Strong: "Offering: logo design in Figma, including editable source files. Need: Excel and Google Sheets training, two hours. Remote. I can deliver within five days."

Specific skill offered, specific skill wanted, timeline stated, remote or local made clear.

Who barter suits, and who it does not

Barter is genuinely good for one thing: getting a real, finished portfolio piece when you have nothing. A finished logo is worth more than three unpaid internships.

Barter is bad for anything urgent, anything revenue-critical, and anything where you need to invoice.

If the work matters to someone paying a customer, barter it. If it matters to your learning, barter it freely.

We weighed the trade-offs more carefully in barter versus freelancing if you have not yet decided.

Bartering is not a shortcut around money

There is a version of bartering where it feels like a clever way to avoid being broke. In practice it is a way to get work you would not otherwise have been able to do, and it will not pay rent.

Used honestly, it is a way to start. Used as a substitute for charging money, it keeps you stuck at the same level while feeling productive.

Questions people ask about barter

Why do barter exchanges usually fail?

Because nothing forces either side to finish. There is no invoice, no payment, and usually no deadline, so the person who is slightly less enthusiastic quietly stops replying. A swap fails at the delivery step far more often than at the matching step.

Is barter worth it when you are starting out?

For a first portfolio piece and a real reference, often yes, provided the scope is small and written down. It is poor value for anything urgent or revenue-critical, because you cannot invoice for it and you have no leverage if the other side stalls.

How do I write a barter request that gets matched?

Name one skill you offer and one skill you need, state a realistic timeline, say whether it is remote or in your city, and describe what finished work looks like. Vague requests are the main reason a request never gets a reply.

What happens after a barter is matched?

Both sides confirm, then each delivers their half. Grit&Gigs tracks delivery per person, so a stalled trade is visible rather than a private argument, and a completed one can be reviewed.

How do you avoid being ghosted in a skill swap?

Agree what done means before you start, split the work into checkpoints, ask for a small commitment from the other side first, and use a platform that records both sides' delivery instead of relying on goodwill.
