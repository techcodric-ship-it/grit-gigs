Interviews Aren't a Knowledge Test — They're a Rehearsal Test

October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

You have read the questions. You know STAR. You have a folder of "interview prep" screenshots you saved at 1 am and never opened again. And somewhere between reading your answer in your head and saying it out loud to a panel, the offer quietly dies.

Here is the uncomfortable truth nobody tells candidates in India: interviews do not test what you know. They test what you can perform under a clock, out loud, with someone watching. Reading is not rehearsal. Rehearsal is rehearsal.

What Actually Happens in the First 90 Seconds

The interviewer asks "tell me about yourself" — and the fate of the round is often decided before the technical question begins. In those 90 seconds they are not collecting your biodata. They are listening for three things:

  • A 60-second intro that lands — who you are, your strongest skill, and one measurable win. Not your life story, not your 10th-board percentage.
  • Two STAR stories on standby — one conflict handled, one deadline missed. Most candidates have vaguely thought about these. Almost none have said them out loud even once.
  • Control under pressure — the pause before your answer is fine. the "umm… so… basically…" for 40 seconds is not.

None of this is knowledge. All of it is rehearsal.

Why Silent Preparation Fails Every Time

Reading your answers in your head feels productive. It is also where most of the damage happens:

  • Your written answer is longer than your spoken answer can be. What reads as "strong" on screen becomes a 3-minute ramble out loud. Panels stop listening at 90 seconds.
  • Filler words live in your mouth, not your notes. You never notice them until a timer and an audience are in the room.
  • Pressure rewrites your answer. The version you rehearsed in your head does not survive the first nervous pause. The version you have said out loud twenty times does.

The fix is not more reading. The fix is a loop: say it, hear it, fix it, say it again.

The 4-Step Rehearsal Loop That Turns Reading Into Offers

1. Drill out loud, daily. Six fresh questions every day — answer them out loud before you read anyone else's answer. Out loud is the point. Two minutes is enough; the muscle you are building is structure under a clock, not essay writing.

2. Research the company before you rehearse the company. Every company has its own flavour — product questions at a product firm, process questions at a services firm, HR round theatre at both. Rehearsing generic answers for a company-specific panel is rehearsing the wrong play.

3. Run a full timed mock, scored. Five questions, in sequence, timed like a real panel — then a score on every answer with the weaknesses named and a stronger version to rehearse. A friend says "it was good." A score says "your STAR story has no result line — add the number."

4. Check your resume against the role you are actually chasing. If your resume does not show what the interview will ask about, you are rehearsing for a role your own CV does not claim.
