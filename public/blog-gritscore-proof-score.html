Your Resume Says “Results-Driven.”
GritScore Shows the Receipts.

November 20, 2026 · 9 min read

Imagine two freelancers with identical resumes. Same degree, same “5 years’ experience,” both calling themselves passionate, results-driven experts. You have one project slot and 30 seconds to choose. There is nothing on either profile that tells you who can actually deliver.

I've sat on both sides of that table. As a buyer, I've stared at two profiles that looked word-for-word identical and had no honest way to pick. As a seller, I've watched people with less raw skill win the job — not because they were better, but because they were better at claiming it.

That's not a talent problem. It's a proof problem. And it's the reason we built GritScore.

The “Claim Economy” Is Broken

Every profile on the internet is made of adjectives. Hard-working. Detail-oriented. Results-driven. Team player. They cost nothing to write and mean nothing to read, because everyone writes them. So buyers have quietly stopped believing any of it — and fallen back on the only signals left: who wrote the slickest profile, who has the biggest follower count, who got lucky with a referral.

Meanwhile the person who actually shipped the work, solved the hard problem and made a client happy has no simple way to show it. Their proof is scattered across a Google Drive link, a WhatsApp screenshot and a former colleague who would vouch for them — if anyone thought to ask.

The freelancer with the weaker resume keeps winning because the stronger worker never learned to package evidence. We thought that was unfair. So we made packaging evidence the whole product.

“Evidence beats claims. Not a hiring verdict. Just receipts, scored.” — the GritScore promise, in one line

So We Built a Score for the Work, Not the Words

GritScore turns the work you've actually done into a shareable Proof Score from 300 to 900. But the number isn't a vibe or an AI opinion. It's deterministic arithmetic on evidence: the same evidence always produces the same score. Nobody is guessing who you are, and nobody can talk the score up with better adjectives.

Here's what counts:

  • Work with a real link or file attached. A shipped project with an openable link is worth far more than a bullet point. A screenshot helps; a referee's confirmation lifts the ceiling on self-reported work.
  • Completed barter work on Grit&Gigs. If you did real work for someone through a skill swap and the match was completed, that counts automatically — no manual claim needed.
  • Reviews from people you genuinely worked with. A star rating and a short note from a real collaborator is proof you showed up and delivered.

Notice what's not in that list: your photo, your college, your follower count, or how nicely you wrote your bio. We deliberately left out anything that measures proximity to proof rather than proof itself.

The Number Isn't the Point. The Receipts Are.

A score on its own is just another badge — and buyers are right to distrust badges. So every GritScore card is built to be flipped open. The person you send it to can scroll through exactly what you did, see the links and files behind each item, and read the reviews, newest first. The score gets their attention. The receipts earn their trust.

There's even an honesty safeguard: until enough of your items carry checked evidence, the public card shows only your tier (Starter, Rising, Solid…) instead of a precise number. A thin profile can't dress itself up as a 900. That's deliberate — it keeps the signal trustworthy for everyone, including you.

To be clear: a GritScore is a self-declared Proof Score. It is not a hiring verdict, background check or credit score. It summarises evidence a person chose to show. We say that on the card itself, because a proof system is only as credible as its honesty about its own limits.

Who This Is For

  • Freelancers tired of sending the same resume into the same silence — attach a card whose evidence travels ahead of you.
  • Job seekers who did the work but can't prove it in a 20-minute interview — hand the panel receipts instead of adjectives.
  • Students & beginners whose first real experience came from a skill barter — turn that swapped work into your first credible proof.
  • Small business owners hiring on a budget — check a person's actual receipts before you trust them with a project.
