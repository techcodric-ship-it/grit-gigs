Before You Apply to One More Job, Run This 15-Minute Company Check

A job post tells you what an employer wants. It rarely tells you what you need to know. Use this five-step check to verify the role, focus your application and walk into the next conversation with better questions.

October 30, 2026 · 7 min read · By Amuthavanan

Another job application takes ten minutes. Finding out what the job actually involves can take much longer. So many candidates solve the wrong problem: they send more resumes when what they need is a clearer reason to apply.

You do not need a six-tab research project for every opening. You do need enough information to answer three questions: Is this role real and current? Can I explain why my experience fits? What do I still need to ask?

Set a 15-minute timer. This is a first-pass filter, not a promise of an interview or a substitute for deeper research on a role you care about.

The 15-minute company check

  1. Minutes 0–3 · VerifyFind the role on an official careers pageCheck the employer's own site and confirm the title, location, employment type and application route. If a third-party listing sends you to an unrelated domain, asks for money to apply, or disagrees with the official page, pause and verify before sharing personal information.
  2. Minutes 3–6 · DecodeTurn the job description into three work outcomesIgnore the long list for a moment. Write down the three things this person will probably need to deliver. Separate must-have requirements from nice-to-haves. This gives you a focused application instead of a resume stuffed with every keyword you can find.
  3. Minutes 6–9 · SourceCheck what the company says about its workLook at official product, team, careers and newsroom pages. Note what the company is building, who it serves and any recent change that matters to the role. Keep facts separate from your interpretation; a company announcement is evidence of what it announced, not proof of what your day-to-day job will be.
  4. Minutes 9–12 · PrepareMatch two examples from your own workFor each of the top responsibilities, choose a project, class assignment, internship, volunteer task or personal build that shows relevant evidence. Use Situation, Task, Action and Result to organize the story. If you have no direct example, say what you have done that is closest and what you would learn next.
  5. Minutes 12–15 · DecideWrite one question and make an honest callWhat is still unclear about the role, team, location, compensation or process? Write one specific question to ask. Then decide: apply, research further, or skip. A deliberate no is better than another application you cannot explain.

Copy this into your notes before you apply:

Role: ____ · Three outcomes: ____ / ____ / ____ · My two examples: ____ / ____ · One question for the recruiter: ____ · Why this role now: ____

What about interview questions and salary signals?

Candidate reports can help you understand what one person experienced, but they are not a guarantee of what your interview will contain. Treat them as prompts for practice, not as a leaked script. The same goes for pay and placement figures: ask how a number was collected, how many records support it and whether the source is clear.

Prefer information that shows where it came from. Official company pages can confirm published facts. Recent news can add context. Candidate-submitted experiences should be labelled as individual reports. Estimates should be called estimates.

Use research to make a sharper application

Once you have the three work outcomes, make the first third of your resume or application speak to them. Put the most relevant project first. Describe what you personally did, the tools you used and the result you can support. Do not copy the job description into your resume and call it a match.

Then prepare a short answer to “Why this role?” A useful answer connects something specific about the work to evidence from your experience and what you want to contribute next. It does not need to flatter the company or pretend you have followed it for years.

The takeaway

Do not let application volume become a substitute for direction. Spend a few minutes checking the source, understanding the work, choosing evidence and writing one real question. Apply when you can explain the match. Keep researching when you cannot.

Your next application does not need a miracle. It needs a reason.

Questions people ask

Is 15 minutes enough to research every employer?

No. It is a quick filter for deciding whether to apply and what to learn next. Spend longer when a role is a serious option, and verify important details directly with the employer.

Should I trust interview questions posted online?

Use them as one person's account, not a promise. Questions vary by role, team and interviewer. Practise the underlying skill and prepare examples you can explain honestly.

Will company research guarantee that I get hired?

No. It cannot guarantee an interview or offer. It can help you avoid applying blindly, ask more useful questions and explain your fit with better evidence.
